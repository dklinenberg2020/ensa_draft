Research

A starter reading list.

A sampling of recent and foundational scholarship on the economics of national security, organized around eight recurring themes: deterrence, the economic theory of war, geoeconomics, health economics and DoD/VA health programs, labor, procurement, public finance and military expenditures, and terrorism. Click a theme to see its papers.

📄
NBER Working Paper 33273

Deterrence Through Response Curves: An Empirical Analysis of the Gaza-Israel Conflict

Eli Berman, Prabin B. Khadka, Danny Klinenberg & Esteban F. Klor · NBER WP 33273 (December 2024)

Models mutual deterrence as interacting response curves in a repeated game, estimated on incident-level data from the Israel-Gaza conflict, 2007–2017. Finds stable, likely Markovian response patterns consistent with incomplete deterrence, and shows Israeli missile defense shifts Gaza’s response curve toward reduced aggression — as theory predicts. Co-authored by ENSA president Eli Berman.

📄
Quarterly Journal of Economics · 2010

Conflict and Deterrence under Strategic Risk

Sylvain Chassang & Gerard Padró i Miquel · Quarterly Journal of Economics, Vol. 125, No. 4, pp. 1821–1858; NBER WP 13964

Shows that weapons unambiguously deter conflict under complete information, but under strategic risk — when parties can’t be sure how the other reads the situation — arming up can have a non-monotonic, sometimes destabilizing effect on peace.

Read the paper →
📄
International Organization · 2025

Tug of War: The Heterogeneous Effects of Outbidding Between Terrorist Groups

Casey Crisman-Cox & Michael Gibilisco · International Organization, Vol. 79, No. 1, pp. 81–116

Models rival groups competing for public support through violence as a dynamic tug-of-war, calibrated to the Hamas-Fatah rivalry. Finds the effects of competition are asymmetric: pressure from Hamas pushes Fatah toward more terrorism, while pressure from Fatah pushes Hamas toward less — so strengthening Hamas raises overall violence, but strengthening Fatah lowers it.

Read the paper →
📚
Book · Oxford University Press · 2024

The Elements of Deterrence: Strategy, Technology, and Complexity in Global Politics

Erik Gartzke & Jon R. Lindsay · Oxford University Press, 2024

Argues that Cold War deterrence theory, built for a bipolar nuclear standoff, doesn’t travel cleanly to a multipolar world of cyber, space, and conventional threats — and reworks the logic of deterrence for that more complex strategic environment.

Read about the book →
📄
Journal of Political Economy · 1975

Strategic Considerations in the Richardson Model of Arms Races

Michael D. Intriligator · Journal of Political Economy, Vol. 83, No. 2, pp. 339–353

Reworks the classic Richardson arms-race model — where each side’s buildup simply reacts to the other’s — into a strategic missile-targeting framework, deriving reaction functions for attacker and defender from underlying weapons technology and showing when an arms race is stable versus destabilizing.

Read the paper →
📄
American Economic Review · 1992

Cooperation, Conflict, and Power in the Absence of Property Rights

Stergios Skaperdas · American Economic Review, Vol. 82, No. 4, pp. 720–739

Builds the foundational contest-success-function model of how parties split resources between productive activity and fighting when no external authority enforces property rights — the workhorse framework later used across the conflict-economics literature, including models of arming and deterrence.

Read the paper →
📄
American Economic Review · 2012

The Strategy of Manipulating Conflict

Sandeep Baliga & Tomas Sjöström · American Economic Review, Vol. 102, No. 6, pp. 2897–2922

Models an incomplete-information conflict game in which a hawkish or dovish “extremist” can manipulate two players toward war or peace by sending a public signal — and shows whether that manipulation succeeds depends on whether the players’ actions are strategic complements or substitutes.

Read the paper →
📄
American Economic Review · 2011

Linking Conflict to Inequality and Polarization

Joan Esteban & Debraj Ray · American Economic Review, Vol. 101, No. 4, pp. 1345–1374

Formalizes why polarization — distinct from simple inequality — predicts conflict intensity: societies split into a few large, internally similar, mutually antagonistic groups generate more war and violence than societies with the same inequality spread more continuously across individuals.

Read the paper →
📄
International Organization · 1995

Rationalist Explanations for War

James D. Fearon · International Organization, Vol. 49, No. 3, pp. 379–414

The foundational bargaining model of war: since fighting is costly, rational states should always be able to find a negotiated settlement both prefer to war — so war itself is the puzzle to explain. Identifies private information with incentives to misrepresent it, commitment problems, and issue indivisibility as the three rationalist mechanisms that can break down bargaining and produce conflict anyway.

Read the paper →
📄
American Economic Review · 2007

Political Bias and War

Matthew O. Jackson & Massimo Morelli · American Economic Review, Vol. 97, No. 4, pp. 1353–1373

Shows that war requires a specific kind of failure — a decision maker whose personal risk/reward ratio from fighting is biased relative to the country’s as a whole — since unbiased leaders could always find a mutually acceptable transfer to avoid war. Explains why countries may rationally select biased leaders anyway, since bias strengthens a leader’s bargaining position.

Read the paper →
📄
International Organization · 2006

War as a Commitment Problem

Robert Powell · International Organization, Vol. 60, No. 1, pp. 169–203

Argues Fearon’s private-information mechanism isn’t the only rationalist path to war: when power is shifting, a rising state cannot credibly commit to honor a deal once it grows stronger, so a declining state may rationally fight a preventive war today rather than accept a bargain it expects to be broken tomorrow.

Read the paper →
📄
Journal of Economic Theory · 2010

A Dynamic Theory of War and Peace

Pierre Yared · Journal of Economic Theory, Vol. 145, No. 5, pp. 1921–1950

Extends the static bargaining model of war into a repeated, dynamic setting with limited commitment and private information, characterizing how the risk of war evolves over time as states learn about each other’s strength and renegotiate the peace.

Read the paper →
📄
NBER Working Paper 31024

International Sanctions and Dollar Dominance

Javier Bianchi & César Sosa-Padilla · NBER WP 31024 (2023, rev. 2025)

Models how the anticipation of financial sanctions erodes the convenience yield on dollar assets, with welfare costs for both the sanctioning and sanctioned economies.

Read the paper →
📄
NBER Working Paper 31852

A Framework for Geoeconomics

Christopher Clayton, Matteo Maggiori & Jesse Schreger · NBER WP 31852 (2023, rev. 2024)

A formal model of geoeconomic power as the ability of a dominant economy to coordinate threats across otherwise unrelated economic relationships — identifying which sectors matter most for enforcing demands on foreign entities.

Read the paper →
📄
Journal of Political Economy · 1992

Sanctions

Jonathan Eaton & Maxim Engers · Journal of Political Economy, Vol. 100, No. 5, pp. 899–928

An early formal model of sanctions as a threat game between a sender and a target: since the sender only imposes costly sanctions if the target refuses to comply, the credible threat — not the sanction itself — does most of the work, and stronger senders paradoxically use overt sanctions less often.

Read the paper →
📚
Book · University of California Press · 1945

National Power and the Structure of Foreign Trade

Albert O. Hirschman · University of California Press, 1945

The founding text of geoeconomics: Hirschman showed that trade between unequal partners creates dependence a stronger state can exploit for political leverage, and proposed a measure of how concentrated a country’s trade was among a few partners — the concentration measure that, independently rediscovered by Orris Herfindahl, became the Hirschman-Herfindahl Index now standard across economics.

Read about the book →
📄
American Economic Review · 1988

The Theory of International Economic Sanctions: A Public Choice Approach

William H. Kaempfer & Anton D. Lowenberg · American Economic Review, Vol. 78, No. 4, pp. 786–793

Reframes sanctions as the outcome of domestic interest-group politics in both the sending and target countries rather than a unified national choice, explaining why sanctions often persist even when they visibly fail to change the target government’s policy.

Read the paper →
📚
Handbook of the Economics of Conflict · 2024

Trade Policy in the Shadow of War: A Quantitative Toolkit for Geoeconomics

Mathias Thoenig · Chapter 8 in O. Dube, M. Morelli & D. Ray (eds.), Handbook of the Economics of Conflict, Vol. 1, North-Holland, pp. 325–380

A quantitative toolkit for the two-way interaction between trade and interstate war, combining a structural gravity model of trade with a diplomatic-negotiation game that can de-escalate tensions and prevent conflict. Applied to EU enlargement to Ukraine and trade sanctions against Russia, finding the war probability is typically tiny but the cost of peace-preserving concessions can be large.

Read the paper →
📄
Journal of Public Economics · 2010

Did Vietnam Veterans Get Sicker in the 1990s? The Complicated Effects of Military Service on Self-Reported Health

Joshua D. Angrist, Stacey H. Chen & Brigham R. Frandsen · Journal of Public Economics, Vol. 94, No. 11–12, pp. 824–837

Uses the Vietnam draft lottery to separate the health effects of military service from the disability-compensation incentives that shape self-reports, finding little overall effect on employment or disability — but a sharp rise in non-work-related disability and employment declines concentrated among low-earning white veterans.

Read the paper →
📄
Journal of Health Economics · 2013

The Psychological Costs of War: Military Combat and Mental Health

Resul Cesur, Joseph J. Sabia & Erdal Tekin · Journal of Health Economics, Vol. 32, No. 1, pp. 51–65

Uses the quasi-random assignment of Army servicemembers to combat units to identify the mental-health toll of combat exposure, finding large increases in PTSD symptoms and other mental illness — with substantial associated healthcare costs even after service ends.

Read the paper →
📄
NBER Working Paper 29877

Invisible Wounds: Health and Well-Being Impacts of Mental Disorder Disability Compensation on Veterans

David Silver & Jonathan Zhang · NBER WP 29877 (2022)

Uses the quasi-random assignment of veterans to VA medical examiners who differ in leniency to estimate the effects of mental-disorder disability compensation, finding that an extra $1,000 a year cuts food insecurity and homelessness while raising healthcare utilization and improving veterans’ trust in and engagement with VA clinicians.

Read the paper →
📄
American Economic Review · 1990

Lifetime Earnings and the Vietnam Era Draft Lottery: Evidence from Social Security Administrative Records

Joshua D. Angrist · American Economic Review, Vol. 80, No. 3, pp. 313–336

Uses the randomized Vietnam-era draft lottery as an instrument for veteran status, finding that white veterans earned roughly 15% less than comparable nonveterans well into the 1980s — a founding application of instrumental variables to military labor economics.

Read the paper →
📄
Econometrica · 1998

Estimating the Labor Market Impact of Voluntary Military Service Using Social Security Data on Military Applicants

Joshua D. Angrist · Econometrica, Vol. 66, No. 2, pp. 249–288; NBER WP 5192

Compares applicants who enlisted to those who didn’t to isolate the effect of voluntary service, finding large earnings gains while in uniform and modest post-service gains for nonwhite veterans — driven mainly by improved civilian employment prospects.

Read the paper →
📄
NBER Working Paper 33382

The Impact of World War II Army Service on Income and Mobility in the 1960s by Ethnoracial Group

Sergio E. Barrera, Andreas Ferrara, Price V. Fishback & Misty L. Heggeness · NBER WP 33382 (January 2025)

Links 1940 Census, enlistment, and 1969 tax records to study how WWII Army service affected income and migration across racial and ethnic groups, finding that once selection is properly accounted for, the earnings and mobility effects of service were uneven — and often negative — across groups.

Read the paper →
📄
NBER Working Paper 7452

Going to War and Going to College: Did World War II and the G.I. Bill Increase Educational Attainment for Returning Veterans?

John Bound & Sarah E. Turner · NBER WP 7452 (1999); published in Journal of Labor Economics, 2002

Uses variation in draft exposure and cohort size across states to net out selection, finding that WWII service and the G.I. Bill produced a moderate net gain in postsecondary educational attainment for veterans.

Read the paper →
📄
Journal of Political Economy · 2024

The Effects of Combat Deployments on Veterans’ Outcomes

Jesse Bruhn, Kyle Greenberg, Matthew Gudgeon, Evan K. Rose & Yotam Shem-Tov · Journal of Political Economy, Vol. 132, No. 8, pp. 2830–2879; NBER WP 30622

The all-volunteer-era counterpart to Angrist’s draft-lottery work: uses quasi-random assignment to combat deployments to show they raise injuries, combat deaths, and disability compensation, but have limited effect on suicide, financial health, incarceration, or education.

Read the paper →
📄
NBER Working Paper 32774

World War II Service and the G.I. Bill: New Evidence on Selection and Veterans’ Outcomes from Linked Census Records

William J. Collins & Ariell Zimran · NBER WP 32774 (2024)

Links individual-level Census records across decades to separate who selected into service from what service caused, finding younger WWII veterans gained in both education and labor market outcomes relative to similar nonveterans.

Read the paper →
📄
American Economic Review · 2026

Competition under Incomplete Contracts and the Design of Procurement Policies

Rodrigo Carril, Andrés Gonzalez-Lira & Michael S. Walker · American Economic Review, Vol. 116, No. 2, pp. 535–581

Studies U.S. defense procurement and finds that inviting more bidders lowers the winning price but worsens post-award performance — more cost overruns and delays. Estimates an auction model with endogenous entry and execution risk, and shows that simple tweaks to how the government publicizes contract opportunities could generate meaningful savings.

Read the paper →
📄
NBER Working Paper 24201

Bureaucratic Competence and Procurement Outcomes

Francesco Decarolis, Leonardo M. Giuffrida, Elisabetta Iossa, Vincenzo Mollisi & Giancarlo Spagnolo · NBER WP 24201 (2018); published in Journal of Law, Economics, & Organization, 2021

Links U.S. federal procurement contract outcomes to the competence of the purchasing bureau, finding that more capable buying offices see significantly fewer delays, cost overruns, and renegotiations.

Read the paper →
📄
Review of Economic Studies · 2022

Winning by Default: Why Is There So Little Competition in Government Procurement?

Karam Kang & Robert A. Miller · Review of Economic Studies, Vol. 89, No. 3, pp. 1495–1556

Estimates a model of costly seller search and contract negotiation on IT and telecom procurement data, finding that drawing in more bidders yields far smaller savings than textbook auction theory suggests, because the buying agency can already extract most of the informational rents from the sellers who do show up.

Read the paper →
📄
Journal of Political Economy · 1987

Auctioning Incentive Contracts

R. Preston McAfee & John McMillan · Journal of Political Economy, Vol. 95, No. 5, pp. 848–872

A foundational model of how a buyer — the canonical example is a defense procurement agency — should auction a contract when bidders differ in cost efficiency and the winning contract mixes fixed-price and cost-reimbursement elements.

Read the paper →
📄
NBER Working Paper 24158

The Arsenal of Democracy: Production and Politics During WWII

Paul W. Rhode, James M. Snyder Jr. & Koleman Strumpf · NBER WP 24158 (2017)

Examines the geographic allocation of over $3 trillion (current dollars) in WWII military supply contracts, finding that industrial capacity — not electoral politics — dominated where contracts landed.

Read the paper →
📄
IMF Working Paper · 2026

Macroeconomics of War and Recovery

Hippolyte W. Balima, Colombe Ladreit, Andresa Helena Lagerborg, Flavien Moreau, Andrea F. Presbitero & Evgenia Weaver · IMF Working Paper (September 2026)

Assembles a new global dataset spanning 194 countries and 170 conflict onsets from 1946–2024 to trace macroeconomic aggregates around the start and end of wars, using local-projection difference-in-differences methods alongside geocoded firm-level evidence. Finds that conflict-driven output losses typically exceed those from banking, currency, and sovereign debt crises, or severe natural disasters.

Read the paper →
📄
Quarterly Journal of Economics · 2011

Macroeconomic Effects from Government Purchases and Taxes

Robert J. Barro & Charles J. Redlick · Quarterly Journal of Economics, Vol. 126, No. 1, pp. 51–102; NBER WP 15369

Builds a century-long series of U.S. tax rates and spending, identified mainly off wartime defense buildups, to estimate a defense-spending multiplier well below one — implying spending crowds out other GDP components — while tax-rate cuts have sizable expansionary effects.

Read the paper →
📄
American Economic Review · 2026

The Price of War

Jonathan Federle, André Meier, Gernot J. Müller, Willi Mutschler & Moritz Schularick · American Economic Review, Vol. 116, No. 3, pp. 791–827

Draws on 150 years of data across 60 countries to estimate war’s macroeconomic toll: a war of average intensity cuts output in the war-site economy by close to 10% and pushes consumer prices up roughly 20%, alongside sharp declines in capital stock, productivity, and equity returns — with damage spilling over to trading partners and neighbors.

Read the paper →
📄
American Economic Review · 2014

Fiscal Stimulus in a Monetary Union: Evidence from U.S. Regions

Emi Nakamura & Jón Steinsson · American Economic Review, Vol. 104, No. 3, pp. 753–792

Uses regional variation in military procurement spending to estimate an “open-economy relative multiplier” of about 1.5 — sidestepping the usual difficulty that national monetary and tax policy confound spending multiplier estimates, since both are uniform across regions.

Read the paper →
📄
American Economic Review · 1997

The Macroeconomic Effects of War Finance in the United States: World War II and the Korean War

Lee E. Ohanian · American Economic Review, Vol. 87, No. 1, pp. 23–40

Compares WWII, financed mainly through debt, with the Korean War, financed mainly through higher taxes, and uses counterfactual simulations to show debt-financing WWII produced much better output and welfare outcomes than a Korean War-style tax-financed approach would have.

Read the paper →
📄
Quarterly Journal of Economics · 2011

Identifying Government Spending Shocks: It’s All in the Timing

Valerie A. Ramey · Quarterly Journal of Economics, Vol. 126, No. 1, pp. 1–50; NBER WP 15464

Builds a narrative measure of defense-spending news — largely war buildups — back to 1939, and shows that because the public anticipates spending before it happens, standard statistical methods that ignore this timing reach opposite conclusions about whether government spending raises or lowers consumption and real wages.

Read the paper →
📄
American Economic Review · 2003

The Economic Costs of Conflict: A Case Study of the Basque Country

Alberto Abadie & Javier Gardeazabal · American Economic Review, Vol. 93, No. 1, pp. 113–132

Introduces the synthetic control method to estimate that the outbreak of ETA terrorism cost the Basque Country roughly 10 percentage points of per-capita GDP relative to a synthetic comparison region — and shows Basque firms’ stock prices reacted to swings in the peace process.

Read the paper →
📄
Journal of Public Economics · 2008

Religion, Terrorism and Public Goods: Testing the Club Model

Eli Berman & David D. Laitin · Journal of Public Economics, Vol. 92, No. 10–11, pp. 1942–1967; NBER WP 13725

Models radical religious rebel groups — Hamas, Hezbollah, the Taliban — as efficient providers of local public goods that use costly sacrifice to screen out potential defectors before risky attacks. Data on Middle East terrorist attacks support the model’s predictions: religious clubs providing benign local public goods are more lethal and turn to suicide tactics more often. Co-authored by ENSA president Eli Berman.

Read the paper →
📄
Journal of Political Economy · 2011

Can Hearts and Minds Be Bought? The Economics of Counterinsurgency in Iraq

Eli Berman, Jacob N. Shapiro & Joseph H. Felter · Journal of Political Economy, Vol. 119, No. 4, pp. 766–819; NBER WP 14606

Tests an information-centric theory of counterinsurgency against unit-level data from Iraq, finding that U.S. reconstruction spending reduced insurgent violence — but only for small-scale projects where local information about insurgents flows to security forces, not large contracts. Co-authored by ENSA president Eli Berman.

Read the paper →
📄
American Economic Review · 2018

The Logic of Insurgent Electoral Violence

Luke N. Condra, James D. Long, Andrew C. Shaver & Austin L. Wright · American Economic Review, Vol. 108, No. 11, pp. 3199–3231

Uses declassified, geolocated microdata on Afghan election-period violence to show insurgents calibrate attacks carefully — avoiding civilian casualties that could trigger backlash — while a novel instrumental-variables approach shows the violence they do commit succeeds in depressing voter turnout.

Read the paper →
📄
American Economic Review · 2008

The Cycle of Violence? An Empirical Analysis of Fatalities in the Palestinian-Israeli Conflict

David A. Jaeger & M. Daniele Paserman · American Economic Review, Vol. 98, No. 4, pp. 1591–1604

Estimates daily reaction functions for Israeli and Palestinian fatalities during the Second Intifada, finding Palestinian violence Granger-causes Israeli violence but not the reverse — and no evidence of the predictable, symmetric “tit-for-tat” cycle the conventional narrative assumes.

Read the paper →
